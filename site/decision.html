The honest answer today is "not yet" — and that is a real answer. The question is binary: sell before the 2030–2035 expiry, or renew. The evidence below is strong enough to tell you which way the weight points, and it is not strong enough to tell you the price. Choosing a price without inputs would be worse than not choosing. So this page gives a conditional recommendation with an explicit sequence, not a verdict.

What the verified evidence says

  • The area is not growing. Metro employment is +1.29% versus October 2019 and +0.19% versus October 2024. City population is −0.8% since 2020. Seven of nine tracked sectors are contracting or flat.
  • The tenant is shrinking. 86 closures in FY2025, guidance for 50–100 more, same-store sales −4.2% then −7.4%, 7.33× leverage, and a stated plan to sell real estate.
  • The tax code rewards acting. Section 1031 deferral exists only if the owner sells a qualifying real-property interest, and it can move the effective tax on reinvested proceeds toward zero.
  • The tail is correlated. A regional catastrophe stops rent, triggers repairs and closes the transaction market at the same moment, so holding a single illiquid asset through a long horizon is punished more than the base case suggests.

What the verified evidence cannot say

  • The rent. Unknown. Neither case can be priced.
  • The term. A five-year range, which cannot be discounted.
  • The obligor. Brand or franchisee — different balance sheets entirely.
  • The basis. Unknown, so the tax layer is unknown.
  • The price. No quote, no comparable, no verified spread.
  • The hazards. No parcel-level zone status for seismic, flood or fire.

Zero of twelve decision-gate items are closed. See decision gates.

Recommended course of action

Conditional, and the condition is sequencing — not a target price.

StepActionWhy this order
1Close every blocking gate (G-01, G-02, G-03, G-04, G-07, G-12). Nothing else happens first.Six unknowns entirely govern the answer.
2Obtain written quotes from three buyers AND a written renewal indication from the tenant, in the same 30-day window.This is the only way to compare a real sale price against a real renewal case rather than two templates.
3Ask the tenant, through the broker, whether it will buy the parcel.D-5 is the path most likely to produce the highest price and the fewest surprises, and it is almost never tested.
4Run the after-tax number with and without a section 1031 exchange, and identify replacement candidates before listing.The tax difference is large enough to change the decision, and the exchange must not be improvised.
5Decide against the owner's written objective function (G-12).Sell and hold optimise different things. That trade-off belongs to the owner and cannot be delegated to an analysis.

The lean, if the owner must choose direction today. On the verified evidence, the weight of argument favours selling — but only after gates 1 and 2, and only with the 1031 path prepared in advance. If the objective statement prioritises income over liquidity and the lease has 8+ years of firm term with a strong obligor and a market-reset option, hold becomes defensible. Those two facts are unknown today, which is precisely why the answer is 'not yet'.

What would flip the recommendation to hold

  • A firm term extending meaningfully past 2032, with no tenant termination right.
  • A corporate or well-capitalised guaranty behind the rent.
  • A renewal option with a fair-market-value reset rather than a fixed bump.
  • Parcel-level hazard findings that are benign, plus affordable insurance.
  • Zoning headroom that gives the land a credible alternative use.
  • Three written quotes that come in materially above the owner's hold-case present value.

Each of these is checkable within weeks. That is what makes the recommendation conditional rather than vague: the conditions are testable, and the tests are listed.

The seven paths, and why most of them lose

A complete enumeration. Any decision the owner makes is one of these — including doing nothing.

Three paths deserve particular attention.

  • D-5, sale-leaseback to the tenant. Almost nobody prices this. A tenant that owns its own rent can rationally pay more than any investor, because it is buying the elimination of its own lease obligation. The verified filing shows the tenant already acquires assets for sale-and-leaseback and has named "selling real estate" in its plan, which makes it a plausible counterparty rather than a hypothetical one.
  • D-6, 1031 exchange. The largest single lever on the after-tax outcome and the easiest to botch. It must be prepared before listing, not after the closing.
  • D-7, do nothing. The default. It looks like caution and functions as a leveraged bet on a tenant that has publicly said it is shrinking.

Why "based on the existing economic activity" points the way it does

The brief asks for the decision to rest on the area's economic activity. Here is that reasoning made explicit, using only verified numbers.

  1. A ground lease is a claim on a tenant's ability and willingness to pay rent. Everything else is secondary.
  2. The tenant's willingness is measured by its own disclosures: closures up, same-store sales negative two years running, leverage above 7×, and a plan that includes selling real estate.
  3. The tenant's ability is measured by its end market: Los Angeles is adding jobs at +0.19% over two years while the operator's wage floor rises automatically and unrestrained by local inflation of 3.7%.
  4. The asset's alternative value is set by land use, not by the rent. That requires zoning headroom (unverified) and construction activity, which is flat at +0.2%.
  5. Therefore the hold case rests on a renewal that the counterparty has given no indication it wants, and on a rent reset that the option mechanism may not permit.

None of this makes selling correct at any price. It makes selling correct if the price clears the owner's threshold, and the threshold is only knowable once the six blocking gates close. That is why the recommendation is a sequence and not a verdict.
