Bottom line

Neither "sell now" nor "renew" is defensible on the evidence that exists today. That is not evasion; it is the finding. Three of the four facts that decide this property are private and unverified, and the public evidence points in both directions at once.

What is defensible today: do not sign a renewal, do not list the property, and do not accept an unsolicited offer, until the five documents on Questions are in hand. That work costs a few thousand dollars and two to three weeks. It is the cheapest part of this decision by a very wide margin, and everything else depends on it.

The case for renewing, stated as strongly as the evidence allows

  • You own the land, not the brand. A Jack in the Box NNN lease is a land play with a tenant attached. If the parcel sits in a transit corridor, the land value may exceed what a burger tenant can pay, and renewal rents are set by the land, not by the tenant's same-store sales.
  • The tenant is stabilising, not collapsing. The quarterly same-store sales trend improved in every quarter of FY2026 (−6.7%, −3.8%, −1.1%). Closures are a deliberate programme to fix franchisee profitability, not a disorderly retreat. A tenant that finishes that programme is a better credit in 2028 than it is today.
  • A sale is expensive. A taxable sale of a long-held property faces federal capital gains, 25% recapture on every dollar of depreciation taken, 3.8% NIIT, and California tax at ordinary rates with no preferential capital-gain rate. Inside the City of Los Angeles, Measure ULA adds 4% of gross price above $5.4M and 5.5% at or above $10.9M, and it is charged on the gross price, not the gain.
  • Selling crystallises a discount you do not have to accept yet. With the lease running to the 2030s there is no forced timeline. A short-lease discount is a price a buyer charges for remaining term; every year you wait the term gets shorter, which cuts both ways — less term, but also less time for the tenant to deteriorate.

The case for selling, stated as strongly as the evidence allows

  • The tenant is shrinking in your market. 86 closures in one year, 2,115 units and falling, with California holding the largest single concentration of Jack in the Box restaurants. A closure programme that removes marginal units can reach a profitable unit if the local trade area weakens.
  • The tenant's cost of capital went up sharply. Refinancing $500M at 7.624% against debt it replaced at 3.4%–4.5% raises the hurdle every store must clear. Rent is a fixed claim ahead of that. A tenant under that pressure negotiates harder at renewal, and the leverage sits with whoever has the alternative.
  • Leadership is in flux. An interim chief executive since May 2026, and a Form 8-K in November 2026 disclosing the departure of the CFO, chief legal officer and CTO across 2027. Renewal negotiations with a rotating counterparty are slower and less predictable.
  • Catastrophic risk is real and priced by insurers, not by you. USGS puts a 60% chance on a magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake in the Los Angeles region within 30 years, and 46% on magnitude 7.0 or greater. A single event can simultaneously damage the building, stop the rent and freeze the market you would need to sell into.
  • Single-tenant, single-site concentration. One lease, one tenant, one parcel, one seismic region. A sale converts a concentrated illiquid position into diversified capital, which is what wealth preservation actually requires.

Weighing the two cases against your stated objective

Your objective is preservation of wealth with room for growth, at a manageable level of risk. Applied honestly to this property, that objective does not point at "sell" or "renew" first. It points at sequence.

ObjectiveWhat renewing does to itWhat selling does to itWhich is better, and why
Preserve capital Keeps the asset and the rent, but keeps 100% of the seismic, tenant and single-site risk. Converts to cash after a heavy tax bill, removing concentration risk. Depends entirely on the after-tax number. Run the calculator with a real basis figure before forming a view.
Grow Growth is capped by a fixed rent stream plus land appreciation; escalations are contractual, not market. Proceeds can be redeployed or rolled into a §1031 replacement, which defers federal and California income tax on the gain. Selling wins on growth only if the replacement asset beats the hold case after tax. That comparison is not built here because no replacement has been identified.
Manage risk Risk is unchanged and undiversified; you can only insure part of it. Risk becomes a tax-timing and reinvestment question instead of a physical-asset question. Selling wins on risk, clearly, unless the parcel's hazard exposure turns out to be low and the tenant renews on strong terms.
Keep options open Renewal with a defined term preserves the option to sell later, including into a transit-corridor bid. A sale closes the land-optionality position permanently; a §1031 can preserve it elsewhere but not here. Renewal wins on optionality, provided the renewal term is short enough and carries no punitive restrictions on assignment or sale.
The honest synthesis. The risk-minimising move and the wealth-maximising move are not the same move, and which one you should take depends on facts that are private: your basis, your other income, whether you need liquidity, and whether the parcel's hazard exposure is high or low. The evidence supports one clear action: renew nothing and sell nothing until the gates clear. If forced to choose today on public evidence alone, the renewal case has the weaker footing, because the tenant's contraction is documented and its cost of capital has risen, while the land-value upside is speculative until a zoning and transit study is done for the actual parcel.
