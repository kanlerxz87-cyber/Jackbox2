Three scenarios, and what moves in each

ScenarioWhat happensWhat the owner can controlWhat the owner cannot
BaseRent is paid through the term; the tenant closes some units elsewhere; the parcel stays insurable at a higher premium. The renewal rent, the timing of any sale, and the quality of the diligence file. Cap rates, the tenant's systemwide performance, and the trade area's direction.
DownsideThe tenant asks for relief or gives notice; the building sits vacant for a year or more; insurance costs rise sharply. Whether a security deposit, guarantee or letter of credit exists at renewal; how fast a re-leasing plan can start. How long a specialised building takes to re-let, and whether a replacement tenant exists at all.
Black swanA damaging earthquake or a fire, combined with an insurance market that will not renew, at the same time as a rate shock. The deductible funding, the loss-of-rent limit, and a written rule against selling into the first 90 days. Everything else, including the price a buyer would pay for a damaged asset.

What "manageable risk" could mean, concretely

The owner asked for a balance between preserving wealth and keeping growth possible, at a manageable level of risk. That cannot be answered in the abstract, but it can be operationalised with three thresholds agreed in advance. They are proposed here, not adopted:

  1. Insured-loss threshold. The largest single uninsured loss the owner could absorb from liquid reserves without selling another asset. If the deductible plus the uncovered downtime exceeds it, the hazard programme is inadequate regardless of the premium.
  2. Downtime threshold. The number of months of zero rent the owner could fund from reserves. Compare it with the re-leasing study's estimate; if the study exceeds the threshold, the hold case is a liquidity bet.
  3. Concentration threshold. The share of the owner's net worth this single parcel represents. Above a stated share, idiosyncratic tenant and hazard risk dominate the portfolio and a sale becomes a diversification decision rather than a price decision.
Why thresholds rather than a score. A weighted risk score would look rigorous and mean nothing, because the weights would be invented. Three thresholds tied to the owner's actual balance sheet are checkable, and they convert "manageable" from an adjective into a test.

Risks this page does not cover

  • Owner-specific legal, estate and family considerations, which belong with counsel.
  • Financing risk on any debt secured by the parcel, which is unknown.
  • Local regulatory change beyond the transfer and wage rules already cited, which would require a planning and zoning review.
  • Tenant brand-specific litigation or food-safety events, which cannot be anticipated from filings.
